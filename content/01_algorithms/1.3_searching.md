## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Algorithm** | A fixed, step-by-step set of instructions for solving a problem. |
| **Search algorithm** | An algorithm for finding an item in a collection, or deciding it is not there. |
| **Target** | The item we are looking for. |
| **List** | A collection of items stored in order, one after another (position 1, 2, 3, …). |
| **Position** | Where an item sits in the list (1st, 2nd, 3rd, …). |
| **Check (comparison)** | Looking at one item and asking "Is this the target?" We count checks to measure speed. |
| **Sorted list** | A list arranged in order, such as smallest to largest or A to Z. |
| **Linear search** | Checking items one by one from the start until the target is found or the list ends. |
| **Binary search** | On a sorted list, checking the middle item and throwing away half of the list each time. |
| **low / high / mid** | In binary search: the first, last and middle positions of the part of the list still being searched. |
| **Worst case** | The situation that needs the most checks, for example when the target is last or not in the list. |

## 1. What is searching?

**Searching** means finding where a particular item is in a collection, or finding out that it is not there.

You do this every day:

- Looking for a friend's name in your phone contacts
- Finding a word in a dictionary
- Looking for your keys in your bag

A computer searches all the time too. When you type a name into a contact list or look up a student ID in a school system, the computer runs a **search algorithm**. A search algorithm is a fixed set of steps for finding an item.

Two key words:

- **Target:** the item we are looking for.
- **Check (comparison):** one time we look at an item and ask "Is this the target?"

We measure how fast a search is by **counting the checks**. Fewer checks means a faster search.

---

## 2. Linear search

### The idea

**Linear search** checks the items **one by one, from the start**, until it finds the target or reaches the end.

> 🧠 Everyday picture: looking for your keys by taking items out of your bag one at a time.

### The steps

1. Start at the first item.
2. Check it: is it the target?
   - **Yes:** stop. The target is found.
   - **No:** move to the next item.
3. Repeat step 2 until the target is found or there are no items left.
4. If you reach the end without finding it, the target is **not in the list**.

### Worked example 1: target is in the list

List (not sorted): `7, 2, 9, 4, 6`
Target: **4**

| Check # | Position | Item | Is it 4? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 7 | No, move on |
| 2 | 2 | 2 | No, move on |
| 3 | 3 | 9 | No, move on |
| 4 | 4 | 4 | **Yes, found!** |

✅ Found at position 4 after **4 checks**.

### Worked example 2: target is NOT in the list

Same list: `7, 2, 9, 4, 6`
Target: **5**

Linear search checks 7, 2, 9, 4 and 6. None of them is 5, and there are no items left.

❌ Not found after **5 checks**. To be sure something is missing, linear search has to check **every** item.

### Key facts about linear search

- ✅ Works on **any** list, sorted or not.
- ✅ Very simple to understand and do.
- ⚠️ Can be slow for big lists. In the worst case (target is last or missing), a list of **n** items needs **n checks**. A list of 1,000,000 items could need 1,000,000 checks.

## 3. Binary search

### The idea

**Binary search** works only on a **sorted** list (smallest to largest). Instead of starting at the beginning, it:

1. Checks the **middle** item.
2. Uses the answer to **throw away half** of the list.
3. Repeats on the half that is left.

> 🧠 Everyday picture: finding a word in a dictionary. You open it near the middle. If your word comes earlier in the alphabet, you ignore the whole second half and only look in the first half.

"Binary" means "two". Each check splits the remaining list into two halves.

### Why the list MUST be sorted

Binary search decides which half to throw away by comparing:

- If the target is **bigger** than the middle item, the target must be on the **right**.
- If the target is **smaller** than the middle item, the target must be on the **left**.

This only works if the list is in order. In an unsorted list, a bigger number could be anywhere, so throwing away half the list might throw away the target.

### The steps

We keep track of the part of the list that is still possible, using two markers:

- **low**: the first position still possible
- **high**: the last position still possible

1. Set **low = 1** (first position) and **high = n** (last position).
2. Find the middle: **mid = (low + high) ÷ 2**, rounded **down** to a whole number.
3. Check the item at position **mid**:
   - If it **equals** the target: stop, found!
   - If it is **smaller** than the target: the target is on the right, so set **low = mid + 1**.
   - If it is **bigger** than the target: the target is on the left, so set **high = mid − 1**.
4. Repeat steps 2–3 while **low ≤ high**.
5. If **low becomes bigger than high**, there is nothing left to check, so the target is **not in the list**.

### Worked example 3: target is in the list

Sorted list of 8 items:

| Position | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Item | 3 | 8 | 12 | 17 | 25 | 31 | 40 | 52 |

Target: **31**

| Check # | low | high | mid = (low + high) ÷ 2, rounded down | Item at mid | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 8 | (1 + 8) ÷ 2 = 4.5 → **4** | 17 | 17 < 31, go right: low = 5 |
| 2 | 5 | 8 | (5 + 8) ÷ 2 = 6.5 → **6** | 31 | **31 = 31, found!** |

✅ Found at position 6 after only **2 checks**. Linear search would have needed **6 checks** (3, 8, 12, 17, 25, 31).

### Worked example 4: target is NOT in the list

Same sorted list. Target: **10**

| Check # | low | high | mid | Item at mid | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 8 | 4 | 17 | 17 > 10, go left: high = 3 |
| 2 | 1 | 3 | 2 | 8 | 8 < 10, go right: low = 3 |
| 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 12 | 12 > 10, go left: high = 2 |
| — | 3 | 2 | — | — | low (3) > high (2): **stop** |

❌ Not found after **3 checks**. Linear search would need all **8 checks** to be sure.

### Key facts about binary search

- ✅ Very fast on big lists, because each check removes about **half** of what is left.
- ⚠️ The list **must be sorted** first.
- ⚠️ Slightly harder to follow than linear search, and it's easy to make mistakes with low, high and mid.

## 4. Comparing the two searches

### How many checks in the worst case?

Each binary search check halves the list. So the question is: **how many times can you halve n until 1 item is left?**

Example with 8 items: 8 → 4 → 2 → 1. That is 3 halvings, plus 1 final check, which makes **4 checks at most**.

| Number of items (n) | Linear search (worst case) | Binary search (worst case) |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | 8 checks | 4 checks |
| 100 | 100 checks | 7 checks |
| 1,000 | 1,000 checks | 10 checks |
| 1,000,000 | 1,000,000 checks | 20 checks |

When the list gets **1,000 times bigger**, linear search needs about **1,000 times more** checks, but binary search only needs **about 10 more**.

> 📌 For the curious: the number of halvings is called **log₂ n** ("log base 2 of n"). Computer scientists say linear search takes **O(n)** time and binary search takes **O(log n)** time. You don't need to calculate logs. Just remember that **halving shrinks a list very fast**.

### Side-by-side summary

| | Linear search | Binary search |
|---|---|---|
| Does the list need to be sorted? | No | **Yes** |
| How it works | Check items one by one from the start | Check the middle, throw away half, repeat |
| Speed on big lists | Slow | Very fast |
| Best for | Small or unsorted lists | Large sorted lists that are searched many times |

### Which one should I use?

- 🛍️ **Unsorted, small:** finding a receipt in a messy pile → **linear search**
- 📒 **Sorted, large:** finding a name in a sorted contact list of 5,000 people → **binary search**
- 🔁 **Unsorted but searched many times:** sort it **once**, then use binary search every time after that.

> ⚠️ **Common misunderstanding:** "Binary search is always faster." Not true! If the target is the **first** item, linear search finds it in 1 check. Also, if the list isn't sorted, binary search can't be used until you sort it, and sorting takes time.

## 5. Optional: what it looks like in code

You do **not** need to program for this course. This is only to show that the steps above turn directly into code (Python). Python counts positions from **0**, not 1.

```python
def linear_search(items, target):
    for position in range(len(items)):
        if items[position] == target:
            return position          # found
    return -1                        # not found

def binary_search(items, target):
    low, high = 0, len(items) - 1
    while low <= high:
        mid = (low + high) // 2      # // means divide and round down
        if items[mid] == target:
            return mid               # found
        elif items[mid] < target:
            low = mid + 1            # go right
        else:
            high = mid - 1           # go left
    return -1                        # not found

numbers = [3, 8, 12, 17, 25, 31, 40, 52]
print(binary_search(numbers, 31))   # 5  (position 6 if you count from 1)
print(binary_search(numbers, 10))   # -1 (not found)
print(linear_search([7, 2, 9, 4, 6], 4))  # 3 (position 4 if you count from 1)
```

## 6. Activity: The Guessing Game 🎲

**You need:** a partner, and paper or your phone's notes.

**Round 1: linear guessing**
1. Your partner secretly picks a whole number from **1 to 100**.
2. You guess 1, then 2, then 3, and so on. Your partner only says "yes" or "no".
3. Count your guesses.

**Round 2: binary guessing**
1. Your partner picks a new number from 1 to 100.
2. Always guess the **middle** of the numbers still possible. Start with 50.
3. Your partner says **"higher"**, **"lower"**, or **"correct"**.
4. Throw away the impossible half and guess the middle of what's left.
5. Count your guesses.

**Record your results:**

| Round | Method | Number of guesses |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Linear (1, 2, 3, …) | |
| 2 | Binary (guess the middle) | |

**Think about it:**
- a) In Round 2, what is the **most** guesses you could ever need?
- b) Why did Round 2 only work because your partner said "higher" or "lower", and not just "no"?
- c) Which round is like linear search, and which is like binary search?

## 8. Answers

**Q1.** Linear search checks items one by one from the start. Binary search checks the middle item of a sorted list and throws away half of the list each time.

**Q2.** The list must be **sorted** (in order).

**Q3.** **50 checks.** Linear search has to check every item to be sure the name is missing.

**Q4.**

| Check # | low | high | mid | Item at mid | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 8 | 4 | 17 | 17 < 52, go right: low = 5 |
| 2 | 5 | 8 | 6 | 31 | 31 < 52, go right: low = 7 |
| 3 | 7 | 8 | 7 | 40 | 40 < 52, go right: low = 8 |
| 4 | 8 | 8 | 8 | 52 | **Found!** |

Found at position 8 after **4 checks**. This is the worst case for 8 items.

**Q5.** **a) 20.** Halving 1,000,000 about 20 times gets you down to 1 item.

**Q6.**
- a) **Binary search.** A dictionary is sorted alphabetically, so you can open it in the middle and throw away half.
- b) **Linear search.** The drawer isn't sorted, so you have to check the socks one by one.

**Q7.** **False.** Binary search is usually much faster on **large sorted** lists. But if the target is the first item, linear search finds it in 1 check. And binary search can't be used at all on an unsorted list unless you sort it first.

**Q8.** The middle item (position 3) is **7**. Since 9 > 7, binary search throws away the left half and searches 4 and 6. But 9 is actually at position 1, in the half that was thrown away. So binary search wrongly reports "not found". This is why the list must be sorted.

**Activity answers:**
- a) **7 guesses** at most. 100 → 50 → 25 → 13 → 7 → 4 → 2 → 1 takes 7 halvings.
- b) "Higher" or "lower" tells you **which half** to throw away. A plain "no" gives you no direction, so you can only remove one number at a time, which is linear search.
- c) Round 1 is **linear search**. Round 2 is **binary search**.

## 9. Summary

- **Searching** means finding an item (the **target**) in a collection.
- **Linear search** checks items one by one. It works on any list but can be slow: up to **n** checks.
- **Binary search** checks the middle and throws away half each time. It's very fast (1,000,000 items need only about **20** checks) but **needs a sorted list**.
- Choose based on the situation: **small or unsorted → linear; large and sorted → binary.**
