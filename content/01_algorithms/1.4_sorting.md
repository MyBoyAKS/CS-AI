## Key terms

| Term | Meaning (one line, plain words) |
|---|---|
| **Algorithm** | A finite, step-by-step set of clear instructions that solves a problem or completes a task. |
| **Sorting** | Putting items in order, for example from smallest to largest. |
| **Ascending order** | From smallest to largest (1, 2, 3, 4). |
| **Comparison** | Checking two items to see which one is bigger. |
| **Swap** | Exchanging the places of two items in the list. |
| **Pass** | One full walk through the list (or through the unsorted part of it). |
| **Bubble sort** | Compare neighbours and swap them if they are in the wrong order, again and again. |
| **Selection sort** | Find the smallest item and move it to the front, again and again. |
| **Insertion sort** | Take the next item and slide it left into its correct place among the items already sorted. |

## 1.4.1 Why does this matter? (the problem)

In Chapter 1.3 you saw that binary search can find a number in a list of 1,000,000 items with only about 20 checks. But it has one condition: **the list must be sorted**. If the list is mixed up, binary search does not work.

Sorting is everywhere. Your phone sorts your contacts by name. A shop sorts products by price. A teacher sorts exam results from highest to lowest. Each time, a computer has to turn a mixed-up list into an ordered one.

So the question of this chapter is: **how can a computer put a list in order, using only small, clear steps?**

## 1.4.2 The idea

A computer cannot look at a whole list and "see" the right order the way you might with three numbers. It can only do one small thing at a time: **compare two items** and, if needed, **swap** them. Every sorting method is a plan for which two items to compare next.

**Everyday analogy: lining up a group of people by height.** There are different ways to do it:

- **Bubble sort:** each person compares heights with the person next to them. If the taller one is on the left, they swap places. Keep walking along the line until nobody needs to swap.
- **Selection sort:** find the shortest person and send them to the front. Then find the shortest of the people left, and send them to the second place. Repeat.
- **Insertion sort:** people arrive one at a time. Each new person walks along the line from the right and stops in their correct place.

**Where the analogy stops working:** a person can glance at the whole line and see who is shortest. A computer cannot. To find the shortest, it has to compare the items one by one, and every comparison counts as a step. That is why we count comparisons and swaps to see which method does less work.

## 1.4.3 How it works, step by step

We use the same pseudocode style as Chapters 1.1 and 1.2. `A[i]` is the item at position i in list A, and position 1 is the first item.

### 1.4.3.1 Bubble sort

1. Walk along the list and compare each pair of neighbours.
2. If the left one is bigger, swap them.
3. After one pass, the biggest item has "bubbled" to the end. It is in its final place.
4. Repeat for the rest of the list. Each pass can stop one place earlier than the last.

```
BUBBLE-SORT(A)
    n ← length(A)
    for pass ← 1 to n - 1
        for i ← 1 to n - pass
            if A[i] > A[i + 1]
                swap A[i] and A[i + 1]
```

### 1.4.3.2 Selection sort

1. Look through the unsorted part and find the smallest item.
2. Swap it with the first item of the unsorted part. It is now in its final place.
3. Repeat for the rest of the list.

```
SELECTION-SORT(A)
    n ← length(A)
    for pos ← 1 to n - 1
        smallest ← pos
        for i ← pos + 1 to n
            if A[i] < A[smallest]
                smallest ← i
        if smallest ≠ pos
            swap A[pos] and A[smallest]
```

Selection sort swaps **at most once per pass**, because it only moves the smallest item it found.

### 1.4.3.3 Insertion sort

1. The first item on its own counts as a sorted part.
2. Pick up the next item.
3. Compare it with the item on its left. If the left one is bigger, move the picked-up item one place left. Keep doing this.
4. **Stop as soon as the item on the left is not bigger.** The picked-up item is now in the right place among the sorted part.
5. Repeat until every item has been picked up.

```
INSERTION-SORT(A)
    for i ← 2 to length(A)
        j ← i
        while j > 1 and A[j - 1] > A[j]
            swap A[j - 1] and A[j]
            j ← j - 1
```

This is how most people sort playing cards in their hand. Step 4 is the important difference: insertion sort can **stop early**, so it often needs fewer comparisons than the other two.

### 1.4.3.4 Counting the work

For a list of **n** items, bubble sort and selection sort always make the same number of comparisons:

> (n − 1) + (n − 2) + ... + 1

| Items (n) | Comparisons for bubble and selection sort |
|---|---|
| 4 | 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 |
| 5 | 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 |
| 10 | 9 + 8 + ... + 1 = 45 |

Insertion sort makes **at most** this many, and often fewer, because of its early stop.

## 1.4.4 Worked example 1: bubble sort on 4, 1, 3, 2

This trace was done by hand with 4 cards. Each row is one comparison.

| Pass | Compare | Swap? | List now |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 and 1 | Yes (4 > 1) | 1, 4, 3, 2 |
| 1 | 4 and 3 | Yes (4 > 3) | 1, 3, 4, 2 |
| 1 | 4 and 2 | Yes (4 > 2) | 1, 3, 2, **4** |
| 2 | 1 and 3 | No | 1, 3, 2, **4** |
| 2 | 3 and 2 | Yes (3 > 2) | 1, 2, **3**, **4** |
| 3 | 1 and 2 | No | **1**, **2**, **3**, **4** |

Bold numbers are in their final place.

**Count:** 6 comparisons (3 + 2 + 1) and 4 swaps. After pass 1 the biggest card (4) has bubbled to the end, so pass 2 does not need to check it again. Each pass is one comparison shorter than the one before.

## 1.4.5 Worked example 2: selection sort and insertion sort on the same list

Same list, 4, 1, 3, 2, so the three methods can be compared fairly.

**Selection sort:**

| Pass | Checks in the unsorted part | Smallest found | Swap | List now |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Start with 4. Is 1 smaller? Yes. Is 3 smaller than 1? No. Is 2 smaller than 1? No. | 1 | Swap 1 and 4 | **1**, 4, 3, 2 |
| 2 | Start with 4. Is 3 smaller? Yes. Is 2 smaller than 3? Yes. | 2 | Swap 2 and 4 | **1**, **2**, 3, 4 |
| 3 | Start with 3. Is 4 smaller? No. | 3 | No swap needed | **1**, **2**, **3**, **4** |

**Count:** 6 comparisons and only 2 swaps.

**Insertion sort:**

| Round | Card picked up | Comparisons | List after the round |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | Is 4 bigger than 1? Yes, move. (Reached the front.) | 1, 4, 3, 2 |
| 2 | 3 | Is 4 bigger than 3? Yes, move. Is 1 bigger than 3? No, stop. | 1, 3, 4, 2 |
| 3 | 2 | Is 4 bigger? Yes, move. Is 3 bigger? Yes, move. Is 1 bigger? No, stop. | 1, 2, 3, 4 |

**Count:** 6 comparisons and 4 moves.

**All three methods compared** (counts checked by hand and with a short program):

| List | Bubble sort (comparisons / swaps) | Selection sort (comparisons / swaps) | Insertion sort (comparisons / moves) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4, 1, 3, 2 | 6 / 4 | 6 / 2 | 6 / 4 |
| 5, 2, 9, 1, 6 | 10 / 5 | 10 / 3 | 7 / 5 |
| 1, 2, 3, 4 (already sorted) | 6 / 0 | 6 / 0 | **3** / 0 |

**What the table shows:**

- **Selection sort** always needs the fewest swaps, because it swaps at most once per pass.
- **Insertion sort** needs the fewest comparisons when the list is already sorted, or almost sorted. Each card only compares with the card on its left and stops straight away if that card is not bigger. Bubble sort and selection sort keep re-checking the whole unsorted part in every pass, even when nothing needs to move.
- On 4, 1, 3, 2, all three happen to need the same number of comparisons (6). This tie is only for this list. On other mixed-up lists insertion sort often needs fewer, for example 7 instead of 10 on 5, 2, 9, 1, 6.

| Situation | Good choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The list is already almost sorted | Insertion sort | It stops early, so each card needs very few comparisons |
| Moving items is slow or difficult (for example, heavy boxes) | Selection sort | It makes the fewest swaps |
| You want the easiest method to explain | Bubble sort | It only ever compares neighbours |

**Extra: an improved bubble sort.** If a whole pass makes no swaps, the list must already be sorted, so you can stop. With this small change, bubble sort needs only 3 comparisons on 1, 2, 3, 4. The basic version in section 1.4.3.1 does not do this.

**Beyond this module:** for very large lists, real computers use faster methods such as merge sort and quicksort. The three methods here are the best place to start because every step is easy to follow by hand.

## 1.4.6 Common misunderstandings

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | Correct idea |
|---|---|---|
| Counting one comparison too many per pass. While writing this chapter, the first guesses were 7 comparisons (bubble sort on 4 cards) and 4 (insertion sort on 4 sorted cards). | 4 cards have only 3 neighbour pairs. In insertion sort, the first card is never picked up, so there are only 3 rounds. | n cards give (n − 1) + (n − 2) + ... + 1 comparisons for bubble and selection sort, and n − 1 rounds for insertion sort. |
| "Bubble sort stops when the list is sorted." | The basic version does every pass, even if nothing is swapped. | It always makes (n − 1) + ... + 1 comparisons. Only the improved version stops early. |
| Mixing up selection sort and insertion sort. | Both build a sorted part on the left, so they look alike. | Selection sort **searches** the unsorted part for the smallest item. Insertion sort **takes the next item** and slides it into the sorted part. |
| "A comparison and a swap are the same thing." | Every step compares, but only some steps swap. | Count them separately. On a sorted list there are still comparisons, but 0 swaps. |
| Checking the end of the list again in bubble sort. | After each pass, the last item checked is already in its final place. | Each pass can stop one place earlier than the one before. |

## 1.4.7 Learner activity: sort 5 playing cards by hand

**Goal:** feel the difference between the three methods by doing them, and count the work each one needs.

**You need:** 5 playing cards with different numbers (or 5 pieces of paper with the numbers 7, 3, 9, 2, 5), paper and a pen.

**Task:**

1. Lay the cards in a row in this order: **7, 3, 9, 2, 5**.
2. **Predict:** how many comparisons will bubble sort need? (Use the pattern in section 1.4.3.4.)
3. Sort the cards with **bubble sort**. Write the list after each pass, and count comparisons and swaps.
4. Put the cards back in the order 7, 3, 9, 2, 5. Sort them with **selection sort**. Count again.
5. Put them back again. Sort with **insertion sort**, the way you would sort cards in your hand. Count again.
6. Fill in this table:

| Method | Comparisons | Swaps or moves |
|---|---|---|
| Bubble sort | | |
| Selection sort | | |
| Insertion sort | | |

7. **Reflect:** which method felt easiest to do by hand? Which one moved the cards the least? Was your prediction in step 2 right? If not, why not?

**Bonus:** start with the cards already sorted (2, 3, 5, 7, 9). Which method finishes fastest? Why?

A sample answer is in section 1.4.9.

## 1.4.8 Check your understanding

1. **Predict.** How many comparisons does bubble sort need for a list of **6** items?
2. **Trace.** Sort **3, 1, 2** with selection sort. Write the list after each pass. How many swaps are needed?

## 1.4.9 Answers and explanations

### Activity (section 1.4.7): sample answer for 7, 3, 9, 2, 5

**Prediction:** 5 cards, so 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 comparisons for bubble sort.

**Bubble sort:**

| Pass | Comparisons | Swaps | List after the pass |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 | 3 | 3, 7, 2, 5, **9** |
| 2 | 3 | 2 | 3, 2, 5, **7**, **9** |
| 3 | 2 | 1 | 2, 3, **5**, **7**, **9** |
| 4 | 1 | 0 | **2**, **3**, **5**, **7**, **9** |

**Selection sort:**

| Pass | Comparisons | Smallest found | Swap | List after the pass |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 | 2 | Swap 2 and 7 | **2**, 3, 9, 7, 5 |
| 2 | 3 | 3 | No swap (already in place) | **2**, **3**, 9, 7, 5 |
| 3 | 2 | 5 | Swap 5 and 9 | **2**, **3**, **5**, 7, 9 |
| 4 | 1 | 7 | No swap (already in place) | **2**, **3**, **5**, **7**, **9** |

**Insertion sort:**

| Round | Card picked up | Comparisons | Moves | List after the round |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | 1 | 1 | 3, 7, 9, 2, 5 |
| 2 | 9 | 1 | 0 | 3, 7, 9, 2, 5 |
| 3 | 2 | 3 | 3 | 2, 3, 7, 9, 5 |
| 4 | 5 | 3 | 2 | 2, 3, 5, 7, 9 |

**Results:**

| Method | Comparisons | Swaps or moves |
|---|---|---|
| Bubble sort | 10 | 6 |
| Selection sort | 10 | 2 |
| Insertion sort | 8 | 6 |

Selection sort moved the cards the least (2 swaps). Insertion sort needed the fewest comparisons (8), because 9 stopped after just one comparison in round 2.

**Bonus (2, 3, 5, 7, 9, already sorted):** insertion sort finishes fastest with 4 comparisons (one per round) and 0 moves. Bubble sort and selection sort still need 10 comparisons each, with 0 swaps.

### Question 1

**15 comparisons.** For 6 items: 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 15. The first pass compares 5 neighbour pairs, and each pass after that is one shorter.

### Question 2

| Pass | Checks | Smallest found | Swap | List after the pass |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Start with 3. Is 1 smaller? Yes. Is 2 smaller than 1? No. | 1 | Swap 1 and 3 | **1**, 3, 2 |
| 2 | Start with 3. Is 2 smaller? Yes. | 2 | Swap 2 and 3 | **1**, **2**, **3** |

**2 swaps** (and 3 comparisons). A common mistake is to swap as soon as you find a smaller number in pass 1 (swapping 3 and 1 before checking 2). Selection sort first checks **every** card in the unsorted part, then swaps once.

### Question 3

**Insertion sort.** The 1,000 old scores are already in order, so when insertion sort picks each one up, it stops after **1 comparison**. Only the new score has to slide left into its place. In total that is roughly 1,000 to 2,000 comparisons.

Bubble sort does not stop early. With the new score there are 1,001 items, so it makes 1,000 + 999 + ... + 1 = **500,500 comparisons**, even though almost nothing needs to move. Selection sort also makes 500,500 comparisons. So insertion sort does a few hundred times less work here.

## 1.4.10 Summary

- **Sorting** puts items in order. It matters because methods like binary search (Chapter 1.3) only work on a sorted list.
- A computer sorts by **comparing two items at a time** and **swapping** them when needed. We measure the work by counting comparisons and swaps.
- **Bubble sort** compares neighbours and swaps them. After each pass, the biggest item has bubbled to the end.
- **Selection sort** finds the smallest item and swaps it to the front. It makes the fewest swaps.
- **Insertion sort** takes the next item and slides it into the sorted part, stopping early. It is the best of the three on lists that are already almost sorted.
- For n items, bubble and selection sort always make (n − 1) + ... + 1 comparisons. Insertion sort makes at most that many, and often fewer.
