## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Accuracy** | The percentage of answers that are correct. |
| **Bias (in data)** | A pattern in the data that treats some people unfairly or does not represent them well. Not the same as the bias number in a perceptron (Chapter 3.2). |
| **Proxy** | An input that indirectly stands for something else, such as a home address standing in for income. |
| **Personal data** | Information about an identifiable person. |
| **Black box** | A system where we can see the input and output but cannot easily see why it gave that answer. |
| **Explainability** | How well a person can understand why a model gave a particular answer. |
| **Language model** | An AI model trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next words. Chatbots such as ChatGPT are built on one (Chapter 3.4). |
| **Hallucination** | When a language model confidently states something false, such as a source that does not exist. |
| **Human oversight** | A real person can check, change or overrule the model's decision. |

## 3.6.1 Why does this matter?

Neural networks are already used in decisions that affect real lives. YouTube uses deep neural networks to choose which videos to recommend to you (Covington and others, 2016). Companies have also tested AI for ranking job applicants, as you will see in 3.6.5.

In Chapters 3.1 and 3.3 we saw that a model learns the patterns in its data. It has no sense of right and wrong. So a very accurate model can still be unfair, and when it causes harm, it is not obvious who should answer for it.

These are not only problems for lawyers. You will use these tools, and you may build them. In this very course, you use AI tools and must check what they give you.

## 3.6.2 The idea

There are four main challenges in this chapter.

| Challenge | The problem in one line | Everyday picture |
|---|---|---|
| **Bias in data** | The model copies unfair patterns from the past. | A new employee who learns only by copying a prejudiced boss. |
| **Privacy** | Models need data, and that data can be about you. | A very helpful stranger who has read all your old messages. |
| **Black box** | Thousands or millions of weights cannot explain a decision in human terms. | A doctor who says "take these pills" but cannot say why. |
| **Responsibility** | When the model is wrong, the developers, the company and the user can each blame someone else. | A car crash where the driver, the maker and the mechanic all point at each other. |

There is a fifth problem you may meet directly: **confident mistakes**. A chatbot can write something false in a confident style, because it produces likely-sounding words and does not check facts. This is often called a **hallucination**. In 2023, in the US case *Mata v. Avianca*, lawyers were fined after submitting court cases invented by ChatGPT.

> ⚠️ Where these pictures stop working: a model is not a person, so it does not have opinions or intentions. It cannot be prejudiced in the human sense. People choose its data, its goal and how it is used. That is why responsibility stays with people.

## 3.6.3 How it works, step by step: five questions to ask about any model

1. **Data:** where did the training data come from? Who is missing from it, and what past unfairness could it contain?
2. **Groups:** what is the accuracy for each group of people, and not just overall?
3. **Privacy:** did the people agree to their data being used? Is more data collected than needed?
4. **Explanation:** can the people affected get a reason for the decision and challenge it?
5. **Responsibility:** who checks the model, who can overrule it, and who answers if it does harm?

The two worked examples below show questions 2 and 1 in action.

## 3.6.4 Worked example 1: 97% accurate, but for whom?

A face unlock model is tested on 1,000 people. The numbers are invented for this example.

| Group | People tested | Wrong answers |
|---|---|---|
| A | 900 | 9 |
| B | 100 | 20 |

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Total wrong | 9 + 20 | 29 |
| Total right | 1,000 - 29 | 971 |
| **Overall accuracy** | 971 ÷ 1,000 | **97.1%** |
| Accuracy, group A | (900 - 9) ÷ 900 = 891 ÷ 900 | **99%** |
| Accuracy, group B | (100 - 20) ÷ 100 = 80 ÷ 100 | **80%** |

The headline says "97% accurate". But for people in group B, the model is wrong **one time in five**.

Why does the overall number look so good? Group A is nine times bigger, so it dominates the average. A likely cause is that group B was also rare in the training data, so the model had less chance to learn from them.

**This has happened in real life.** The *Gender Shades* study (2018) tested commercial systems that classify gender from face images. Error rates reached up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women, but at most 0.8% for lighter-skinned men.

## 3.6.5 Worked example 2: a model learns an old bias

A company trains a hiring filter on its past decisions. This small data set is invented, and we use the word score method from Chapter 3.1. Imagine the old managers preferred people from the North district, whatever their skills.

| Hired | Not hired |
|---|---|
| `coding maths north` | `coding maths south` |
| `coding north` | `coding south` |
| `maths north` | `maths south` |

**Word scores (hired minus not hired):**

| Word | Hired | Not hired | Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| coding | 2 | 2 | **0** |
| maths | 2 | 2 | **0** |
| north | 3 | 0 | **+3** |
| south | 0 | 3 | **-3** |

Look at what it learned. **Skills count for nothing, and only the district matters.**

**Decision rule (the same as in Chapter 3.1):** add the word scores of an applicant. A total **greater than 0** means hired. Otherwise, rejected.

| Applicant | Sum | Total | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| `coding maths south` | 0 + 0 - 3 | -3 | Rejected ❌ |
| `maths north` | 0 + 3 | 3 | Hired |

The applicant with **more** skills is rejected. The model did exactly what we asked: it copied past decisions. The fault is in the data and in the goal we gave it.

**Can we just delete the word "south"?** Not necessarily. Other inputs can act as a **proxy**: a school name or a bus route may reveal the same thing. The model can find the old pattern through them.

**This has also happened in real life.** Reuters reported in 2018 that Amazon dropped an experimental recruiting tool. It was trained on ten years of CVs, mostly from men, and it learned to mark down CVs containing the word "women's". According to the report, recruiters looked at its rankings but never relied on them alone.

## 3.6.6 Privacy and the black box

### Privacy

- **Collecting:** training data often comes from photos, messages and web pages. The people in it may never have been asked.
- **Remembering:** research has shown that large models can sometimes repeat pieces of their training data, including personal details.
- **Sharing:** whatever you type into an online AI tool may be stored by the company. Never paste in a friend's private information or passwords.
- **Laws:** many places now have rules on personal data, such as the GDPR in the EU (2016) and the Personal Information Protection Law in China (2021).

### The black box

In 3.4.5 we counted 101,770 learned values in a small network. If that network rejects you, its only "reason" is arithmetic across all those numbers. That is not a reason a person can use.

This matters most when decisions are serious. Say a bank's model refuses you a loan. You should be able to ask why, and fix any mistake in the data. Researchers work on **explainability** methods. For example, a "saliency map" highlights which pixels of a picture mattered most for the model's answer (Simonyan and others, 2014). These help, but they only give a partial view.

A practical rule: **the more serious the decision, the more explanation and human oversight it needs.** A bad film suggestion costs little. A wrong medical or court decision can change a life.

## 3.6.7 Common misunderstandings

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | Correct idea |
|---|---|---|
| "Computers are objective, so models are fair." | A model learns from data made by people, and it copies their patterns. See 3.6.5. | Models can repeat human bias, at a much larger scale. |
| "97% accuracy means it works well for everyone." | In 3.6.4, group B was at 80%. | Check accuracy for each group. |
| "Remove the sensitive input and the bias is gone." | Other inputs can act as a proxy. | Test the results for each group. |
| "If the AI made the mistake, the AI is responsible." | A model cannot be blamed or held to account. | People and organisations are responsible for how it is built and used. |
| "If a chatbot sounds confident, it is probably right." | Confidence is only the style of the text. | Check any facts and sources yourself. |
| "This 'bias' is the same as the bias in a perceptron." | One is a number added in the sum (3.2). The other is unfair patterns in data. | Same word, different meanings. |

## 3.6.8 Learner activity: who is responsible? ⚖️

**Goal:** apply the numbers and the five questions from 3.6.3 to a realistic case, then defend a decision.  
**You need:** paper and a pen. Works best in groups of 3 or 4, but you can do it alone.

**The case (invented).** A hospital buys a model that sorts emergency patients. It says "urgent" or "can wait". The company tested it and reports "93% accurate". It was trained mostly on data from city hospitals. Nurses are told to follow the model unless they have a strong reason not to. One night, a patient from a rural area is marked "can wait", waits three hours, and becomes seriously ill.

### Part A: look at the numbers

| Group | Patients tested | Wrong answers |
|---|---|---|
| City patients | 800 | 40 |
| Rural patients | 200 | 30 |

1. Work out the overall accuracy and the accuracy for each group.
2. **Predict** what would happen to the rural accuracy if the hospital used more rural data in training. Why?

### Part B: take a role

Choose a role (or take turns): the **company**, the **hospital manager**, the **nurse** or the **patient's family**.

3. From your role, write two sentences: what should have been done differently?
4. Answer the five questions from 3.6.3 for this case.

### Part C: decide

5. Share out **100 "responsibility points"** between the company, the hospital manager, the nurse and the model. More points means more of the blame. For example, 50 points means you think they carry half of it. Justify each number in one sentence.
6. Write **two** changes that would make this system safer.

**Record:** your calculations in Part A, your points in Part C with reasons, and your two changes.

*(Answers and expected points are in 3.6.10.)*

## 3.6.9 Check your understanding

**Q1.** A model is tested on 500 people from group A (10 wrong) and 50 people from group B (10 wrong). Work out the accuracy for each group, and overall (to one decimal place).

**Q2.** Using the word scores in 3.6.5, what total does the applicant `coding north` get? What about `coding maths south`? What does this show?

**Q3.** Give one example of a proxy, and say what it might stand in for.

**Q4. Find the error.** A student says: "ChatGPT gave me this reference with an author, year and journal, so it must exist." What should the student do, and why?

**Q5.** Which decision needs the most human oversight?
a) Suggesting a song  b) Sorting spam  c) Refusing a loan  d) Choosing a phone wallpaper

**Q6. True or false:** "When a model gives a harmful answer, the model itself is legally and morally responsible." Explain.

## 3.6.10 Answers and explanations

**Part A.**

1. Overall: (1,000 - 70) ÷ 1,000 = **93%**. City: 760 ÷ 800 = **95%**. Rural: 170 ÷ 200 = **85%**. So a rural patient is three times as likely to get a wrong answer (15% against 5%).
2. It would **probably go up**, because the model would learn from more cases like theirs, as long as the new data is good. It is not guaranteed, so the team must test it again.

**Part B and Part C have no single right answer.** A good answer gives reasons and includes these points:

- **Company:** reported only one overall number and trained on mostly city data. It should have tested each group and said clearly where the model is weak.
- **Hospital manager:** bought the model and set a rule that discourages nurses from overruling it. It should have tested the model on its own patients.
- **Nurse:** was the human present, but was told to follow the model. Blaming only the nurse ignores that pressure.
- **The model:** should get **0 points**. It is a tool that cannot be held responsible. If you gave it points, think about who would pay for the harm.
- **Five questions:** data (mostly city patients), groups (rural 85%), privacy (patient records need permission and protection), explanation (the patient and the nurse got no reason), responsibility (not clear at all, and it should have been agreed in advance).
- **Possible changes:** add more rural data and report accuracy by group; make the model advise while the nurse decides; show a reason or warning when a patient is unlike the training data; review every "can wait" decision after a set time; keep a record so that errors can be investigated.

**Q1.** A: 490 ÷ 500 = **98%**. B: 40 ÷ 50 = **80%**. Overall: 530 ÷ 550 = **96.4%**. The overall number hides group B's result.

**Q2.** `coding north`: 0 + 3 = **+3**. `coding maths south`: 0 + 0 - 3 = **-3**. The applicant with fewer skills scores higher, because the model only learned the district.

**Q3.** Examples: a home address can stand in for income or ethnic background; a list of hobbies or a school name can stand in for gender or wealth. Other reasoned examples are fine too.

**Q4.** Look the reference up in a real library or database. A language model can produce realistic but false references, and a neat format proves nothing.

**Q5.** **c) Refusing a loan.** It seriously affects a person's life, and they need a reason and a way to challenge it.

**Q6.** **False.** A model cannot be held responsible. The people and organisations that build, sell and use it are the ones who answer for it. Exactly how responsibility is shared between them is still debated.

## 3.6.11 Summary

- A model copies patterns from its data, including unfair ones. **Accurate does not mean fair.**
- Always check **accuracy for each group**. One overall number can hide serious problems.
- Removing a sensitive input may not remove the bias, because of **proxies**.
- **Privacy:** data about people needs care. **Black box:** huge numbers of weights give no human reason. Serious decisions need explanation and **human oversight**.
- **Responsibility stays with people.** Check what AI tells you, because confident does not mean correct.
